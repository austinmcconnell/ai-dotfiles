# Tool & Library Selection

How to evaluate and recommend tools, libraries, or technical options. Covers three phases: widen the
field, judge on merit, present the result honestly.

## Best-in-Class First

Pick the best tool for the job. Language or stack familiarity is a **tiebreaker**, not an override.

- When two options are genuinely close **and** one aligns with the project's primary language or
  existing stack, that alignment is a legitimate point in its favor — let it tip a close call.
- Never invoke "it's in our main language" to override a clearly superior tool. A best-in-class end
  experience (especially in a user-facing layer) beats stack loyalty.
- State the tradeoff honestly when the best tool is *not* in the preferred stack — name the strong
  reason, don't quietly pick the familiar option or the unfamiliar one.

## Widen Before Narrowing

Do an exhaustive scan of the landscape before scoring. Treat any inherited or pre-narrowed candidate
list as a *starting point*, not the boundary of the search.

- Find what else exists and what is currently recommended as best-in-class — not just the obvious
  names.
- Research the surrounding context too: domain best practices, common gotchas, and real case
  studies, not only the tools themselves.
- Only after widening should you narrow to a scored comparison.

## Present Ranked Options — Sized to the Actual Field

Match the number of recommendations to how close the field actually is. Do not distort the shape of
the evidence in either direction.

- **When several options are genuinely competitive:** present up to ~3 ranked contenders with their
  tradeoffs and let the user make the call. Do not force a single winner by a razor-thin margin
  ("wins by .02") — the tradeoff analysis is more useful than an artificial verdict.
- **When one option clearly dominates:** give a single recommendation. Do **not** manufacture two
  more contenders for symmetry — padding a clear decision with weak alternatives is as misleading as
  forcing a single winner on a close one.
- The rule is "let the evidence set the count," not "always give three." The same evaluation may
  yield three ranked options for one decision and one clear pick for another.
