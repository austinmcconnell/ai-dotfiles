# Analytical Discipline

How to reason about claims, evidence, and conclusions — whether auditing an outside article or
presenting your own analysis. These are always-on principles; for the full auditing workflow, load
the `stress-test-analysis` skill.

## Core Principles

- **Tag epistemic status.** Every load-bearing statement is one of: verified fact, reported claim,
  inference, speculation, or opinion. Do not present speculation in the voice of fact — the register
  (flat declarative prose, specific numbers, no hedging) must match the actual confidence.
- **Demand the counterfactual for causal claims.** "X, then Y, and X is bad" is correlation on a
  timeline, not cause. Before accepting "X caused Y," ask whether the mechanism is plausible,
  whether the attribution is exclusive (what else could cause Y?), and what Y would look like
  without X.
- **Match precision to methodology.** Specificity that exceeds the disclosed method is rhetoric, not
  analysis. A dated, numeric point-forecast with no model, error bars, or base rate is decoration —
  treat narrow ranges as a signal of overconfidence, not authority.
- **Check falsifiability.** A claim compatible with every possible outcome ("if nothing changes,
  this happens") cannot be evidence. Ask what observation would prove it wrong; if there is none, it
  is a narrative, not a prediction.
- **Count the load-bearing sources.** If removing one source collapses the thesis, that is a single
  point of failure — flag it. An op-ed asserting a conclusion is a claim, not evidence for it.
- **Steelman before critiquing.** Engage the strongest version of a view, not a convenient weak one.
  A one-directional ledger — every data point cutting the same way, no serious counter-scenario — is
  advocacy, not analysis.
- **Tone is a tell, not an argument.** Where an author reaches for contempt or loaded language,
  supply skepticism: each loaded term is a place where an argument should be.

## Applies To Your Own Output Too

When you present analysis, comparison, or a recommendation: separate fact from inference, state what
you verified versus assumed, give counterfactuals for causal claims, and size confidence to the
evidence. Verify load-bearing facts before asserting them — do not launder training-data recall or a
sub-agent's summary into a stated fact (see `research-delegation` steering).
